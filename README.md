# Manifesto

We are starting something, and we don’t have all the answers.

For about three thousand years, Jews have argued with each other about what to do with an inherited tradition. They argued about the text, the calendar, the food, the law, and what any of it meant. The arguing is the tradition. Somewhere along the way, most of the world decided religion was either a set of claims you must accept or a set of claims you must reject. We think that’s a false choice.

Religion is one of the oldest technologies humans have. It taught people to gather every week, to bury their dead with care, to feed strangers, to mark a birth, to sit with grief, to tell the difference between a good year and a hard one. It built memory across generations before anyone had writing, and later it built the writing. Throwing all of that out because some of its explanations turned out to be wrong is like abandoning medicine because of leeches.

So we are keeping it, and we are changing it.

## What we believe?

**We don’t believe in God.** We aren’t going to pretend the question is open to keep the peace. We hold that the universe is natural, that people wrote the texts, and that meaning is something we make together and not something delivered from above. If you believe in God privately, you are welcome in the room, but belief isn’t what we are organized around and nothing in our practice will depend on it.

**We follow the evidence.** When science and tradition disagree about how the world works, science wins. This isn’t a hard rule for us, it’s the only honest option. Tradition keeps its authority where it was always strongest: how to live, how to remember, how to be with each other.

**It’s open source.** Every text, ritual, calendar, song, and commentary we produce is free to copy, modify, translate, and republish. We’ll keep it in public repositories with version histories, because the history of a change is part of the tradition too. Fork it. Improve it. Send it back or don’t.

**The door is open to non-Jews**. This one matters to us, and we want to say it carefully. Judaism gave us the structure and the stories, and we owe that debt openly. But we are not building a club with a bloodline requirement. If you are Jewish by birth, by conversion, by culture, or by none of those and you just want to take part, you can contribute here, propose changes, and run your own version. Jews have always had a complicated relationship with outsiders who wanted in. We’d rather err on the side of welcome.

## How change works

Anyone can propose a change. A proposal is just a written suggestion: here’s what I’d change, here’s why. Others can support it, challenge it, or adapt it. If a community adopts it, it becomes part of how they practice. If thousands of communities adopt it, it becomes common practice. No vote has to be held and nobody needs to sign off.

You can also make a change just for yourself. Skip a practice that means nothing to you. Invent one that does. A personal version of this religion is as legitimate as a shared one. The shared parts exist so you don’t have to start from nothing, not so you can be told what to do.

## What we keep

The skeleton. Every religion, however different on the surface, is built from the same few parts: a calendar, a weekly pause, rites for birth and death and growing up, a body of stories, a set of obligations to each other, and a habit of gathering. These parts survived thousands of years because they work. We keep the frame and take everything else off it.

The structure of the year. Seasons, anniversaries, and days of remembering stay. What changes is what they stand for. A harvest festival can mark the harvest. A holiday about a hard chapter of history can mark that history, with the sources checked and the uncertainty stated.

The rituals, rebuilt. A naming ceremony, a coming of age, a wedding, a funeral: each one stays as a human custom, with its words rewritten so that every sentence is something we mean and can defend.

The obligations to each other. Feeding the hungry, caring for the sick, protecting the stranger. We don’t need a commandment to justify these, and we’d keep them anyway.

The arguing. Commentary, dispute, and footnotes on footnotes. Every text is open to challenge, including this one.

## What we let go

Anything that can’t be checked. Supernatural claims, divine authorship, reward and punishment after death. Where a tradition makes a claim about how the world works, we test it, and if it fails, it goes.

Authority without evidence. Nobody is holier, closer to the truth, or more qualified to decide because of a title or a bloodline. An idea gets weight from its reasoning and its results.

Rules whose only reason is that they’re old. Every practice should be able to answer “why do we do this?” If the only answer is “because we always have,” it goes into the archive, not the practice.

Harm labeled as holy. If a practice hurts people, calling it sacred doesn’t protect it.

## What we’re not

We aren’t a political movement, a nation, or a cult. We don’t have a leader, we don’t take money for membership, and we don’t promise answers about death or the meaning of life. We promise a place to ask, and a set of practices that have kept people company for a very long time.

## Join us

Take this text and change it. Put your name on your version. Translate it into your language. Tell us where we got it wrong.

The oldest Jewish habit is to take a text you were handed and argue with it until it’s yours. We’re just doing that in public.
